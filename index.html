TikTok Upload Prep

Work out the right export settings, check your file against them, and produce the upload — on an iPhone, without a computer.

TikTok re-encodes every video it receives. That is done on their servers, after your upload arrives, and nothing on your end can turn it off. This page does not claim otherwise and cannot bypass it.

What it does is control what their encoder is given. A file at the right resolution and frame rate, with enough bitrate and no prior compression, comes through one encode almost unchanged. A file that has already been squeezed by a phone app, then edited inside TikTok, has been through three or four. That difference is most of what people mean when they say their uploads look bad.

Your video stays on your device. There is no server behind this page and no upload endpoint. The analysis and the conversion both run in your browser. You can turn off your connection after the page loads and the analyzer still works.

      Which of these do you need? Only one.

      • Editing in an app? Method A. Its export dialog is the right tool, and re-encoding its output afterwards only costs you quality.
      • Clip already finished, no editor? Method B.
      • Need the frame rate changed and nothing else will do it? Method C.

      If you are unsure whether your editor is good enough: export a short test with its highest settings, then run it through step 2. The bitrate it reports is the answer. Low bitrate means your editor is the bottleneck, and nothing further down this page can recover detail it has already discarded.

      A. Export from your editing app

      Best, if you have one

      Use this if you are editing in an app with a real export dialog — LumaFusion, VN, Premiere, Resolve, Final Cut. Set these fields:

      Set your project frame rate rather than converting afterwards. An editor conforms high frame rate clips to the project timeline as it renders, which is cleaner than re-encoding a finished file. For slow motion, use the editor's own retiming rather than the Speed setting in method B.

      Export to Files, not Photos. iCloud Photos does not re-compress the originals in your library, but several routes out of Photos do — Shared Albums compress heavily, and share-sheet exports can transcode without telling you. Sending the export straight to Files and uploading from there avoids the question entirely.

      Apps like CapCut, InShot and similar offer only a resolution and a quality slider, and compress hard whatever you choose. Pick their highest settings, then check the result in step 2 so at least you know what you are working with.

      B. Shortcuts app

      No editor needed

      Use this if your clip is already a finished file and you have no editor to re-export it from. Shortcuts is built into iOS and its Encode Media action uses the same hardware encoder as the Camera app, so it handles 4K and long clips without running out of memory.

        Step 4

        Upload from your iPhone

        The web uploader accepts larger, higher-bitrate files than the app's picker, and it skips the compression the app applies before sending. You can reach it from Safari.

        1. Turn on high quality uploads first. In the TikTok app: Profile → menu → Settings and privacy → Content preferences (called Data Saver in older builds), and enable High quality uploads. Turn Data saver off. This is per-account, it applies to web uploads too, and it is off by default. It is the single biggest free improvement available to you.
        2. Open the desktop site. In Safari, go to tiktok.com/upload, then tap aA in the address bar and choose Request Desktop Website. Log in if it asks.
        3. Pick the file you made. Tap the upload area. In the picker choose Browse to reach Files, or Photo Library if you saved it there. Pick the converted file, not the original camera clip.
        4. Caption and post. Do not add text, stickers, filters, effects or trims — each one makes TikTok re-encode the whole video again on top of the encode you cannot avoid. Put your text in the post caption instead. If you want a trending sound, add only the sound, and do not trim afterwards.
        5. Do not save to drafts. Saving and reposting runs another encode. Upload, caption, post.
        6. Judge it later, not immediately. For the first several minutes TikTok often serves a low-quality placeholder while processing finishes. Check again after fifteen minutes or so before concluding anything went wrong.

        Step 5

        Notes

        Why can't I upload without compression?

        Because the re-encode happens after your file reaches TikTok. They transcode every upload into their own set of streaming versions so it can play on slow connections and on any device. There is no account setting, file format or upload route that skips it. Anything advertising otherwise is either mistaken or lying. The realistic goal is one generation of loss starting from a clean source, rather than several.

        What happens to 120fps?

        TikTok plays back at 60fps at most, and a good deal of delivery is 30fps. Your extra frames are not preserved. That is not a reason to stop shooting 120 — it gives you clean slow motion and a shorter shutter, which is real — but it does mean you should decide what those frames become before uploading. Converting deliberately gives a predictable result; letting TikTok's transcoder decide gives you whatever its frame-dropping produces.

        Is uploading 4K worth it over 1080p?

        Usually yes, when uploading through the web uploader. Viewers are not served true 4K, but a 4K source gives the encoder more information per delivered pixel, so the 1080p it produces holds detail better — hair, fabric, foliage, small text. The cost is upload time. A high bitrate 1080p file is a perfectly respectable second choice, and through the phone app it is actually the better choice, because the app downscales locally before sending anyway.

        Which method should I actually use?

        If you are editing in an app, method A — its export dialog produces the file in one encode, and putting that output through B or C afterwards just adds a second. Use B when the clip is already finished and you have nothing to re-export it from. Use C only when you specifically need the frame rate changed, since Shortcuts cannot do that in most iOS builds.

        If you are not sure whether your editor is good enough, export a short test at its highest settings and run it through step 2. The bitrate it reports tells you directly.

        My clip was shot in Slo-Mo mode rather than 120fps video

        Then the slow-motion ramp is part of how Photos presents it, and exporting can bake in whatever the current ramp is. Open the clip in Photos, tap Edit, and check the speed handles under the timeline before exporting so you know what you are getting. If you want the full 120fps to work from, shoot in the regular video mode at 120fps instead of Slo-Mo.

        Why does my video look fine on my phone and bad in the feed?

        In rough order of likelihood: high quality uploads is off in your settings; the clip was edited inside TikTok after uploading; you are looking at the placeholder encode in the first few minutes; or the source file had already been compressed before you uploaded it. Step 2 will tell you whether the last one applies.
